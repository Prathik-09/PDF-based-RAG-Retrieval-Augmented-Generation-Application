COVARIANCE VS CORRELATION

1.  OVERVIEW

Covariance and correlation are statistical measures used to describe how
two variables change in relation to each other.

Both help answer a question such as: “When one variable changes, does
the other variable tend to change too?”

The main difference is: - Covariance describes the direction of the
relationship, but its value depends on the units and scale of the
variables. - Correlation describes both the direction and strength of a
linear relationship using a standardized value between -1 and +1.

2.  WHAT IS COVARIANCE?

Covariance measures whether two variables tend to move together or in
opposite directions.

Interpretation: - Positive covariance: The two variables tend to be
above or below their means at the same time. They generally move in the
same direction. - Negative covariance: When one variable is above its
mean, the other tends to be below its mean. They generally move in
opposite directions. - Covariance near zero: There is little or no
linear co-movement indicated by the covariance. This does not prove that
the variables are independent; a nonlinear relationship may still exist.

Population covariance formula:

Cov(X, Y) = E[(X - mean(X))(Y - mean(Y))]

Sample covariance formula:

s_xy = sum((x_i - mean(x))(y_i - mean(y))) / (n - 1)

Where: - X and Y are the two variables. - x_i and y_i are paired
observations. - mean(X) and mean(Y) are their sample means. - n is the
number of paired observations. - The sample formula uses n - 1 in the
denominator.

Important properties: - Covariance is symmetric: Cov(X, Y) = Cov(Y,
X). - Covariance of a variable with itself is its variance: Cov(X, X) =
Var(X). - Covariance is affected by the units and scale of both
variables. - Its magnitude is therefore difficult to interpret on its
own. - Changing the units of a variable changes the numerical
covariance.

3.  WHAT IS CORRELATION?

Correlation measures the strength and direction of the linear
relationship between two variables.

The most common measure is the Pearson correlation coefficient.

Pearson correlation formula:

r = Cov(X, Y) / (standard_deviation(X) * standard_deviation(Y))

For a sample:

r = s_xy / (s_x * s_y)

Where: - s_xy is the sample covariance. - s_x is the sample standard
deviation of X. - s_y is the sample standard deviation of Y.

The Pearson correlation coefficient always lies between -1 and +1.

Interpretation: - +1: Perfect positive linear relationship. - A value
close to +1: Strong positive linear relationship. - 0: No linear
relationship. - A value close to -1: Strong negative linear
relationship. - -1: Perfect negative linear relationship.

A correlation of zero does not necessarily mean there is no
relationship. It means there is no linear association measured by
Pearson correlation. A nonlinear relationship may still exist.

4.  DIFFERENCE BETWEEN COVARIANCE AND CORRELATION

Covariance: - Indicates whether two variables tend to move in the same
or opposite directions. - Can be positive, negative, or zero. - Has
units equal to the product of the units of the two variables. - Is not
restricted to a fixed numerical range. - Its magnitude is affected by
measurement scale. - Is harder to compare across datasets with different
units or scales.

Correlation: - Indicates the direction and strength of a linear
relationship. - Ranges from -1 to +1. - Has no units; it is
dimensionless. - Is standardized using the standard deviations of both
variables. - Is not changed by positive changes in measurement units. -
Is easier to compare across different datasets.

5.  SIMPLE EXAMPLE

Suppose a teacher records study hours and exam scores for five students.

In this example, students who study more hours generally receive higher
exam scores.

The covariance is positive because study hours and exam scores tend to
increase together.

The Pearson correlation is also positive and close to +1 because the
observations show a strong positive linear relationship.

The exact covariance depends on the units used for study hours and exam
scores. The correlation remains the same if study hours are converted
from hours to minutes, provided the conversion is a positive scale
change.

This example shows: - Covariance tells us the direction of
co-movement. - Correlation tells us the direction and standardized
strength of the linear relationship.

6.  WHY CORRELATION IS EASIER TO INTERPRET

Imagine one dataset measures height in centimeters and weight in
kilograms. Another measures height in inches and weight in pounds.

The covariance values will differ because the measurement units differ.

The correlation coefficient will remain unchanged under positive unit
conversions. This makes correlation more suitable for comparing the
strength of linear relationships across variables measured in different
units.

7.  IMPORTANT: CORRELATION DOES NOT MEAN CAUSATION

A correlation between two variables does not prove that one variable
causes the other.

For example, ice cream sales and swimming incidents may both increase
during hot weather. This does not mean ice cream sales cause swimming
incidents. Temperature may be a third variable influencing both.

When interpreting correlation, consider: - Confounding variables. -
Reverse causality. - The possibility of coincidence. - The quality and
size of the dataset. - Whether the relationship is linear.

8.  WHEN SHOULD YOU USE EACH MEASURE?

Use covariance when: - You need to understand the direction of joint
variation. - You are working with covariance matrices in statistics or
machine learning. - You need covariance as an intermediate calculation,
such as when calculating correlation. - You are studying portfolio
variance or multivariate statistical models.

Use correlation when: - You want an easy-to-interpret measure of linear
association. - You want to compare relationships across variables with
different units. - You are performing exploratory data analysis. - You
want to identify variables that may be strongly linearly associated.

9.  PEARSON CORRELATION AND OTHER TYPES OF CORRELATION

Pearson correlation measures linear association and is commonly used for
numerical variables when its assumptions are appropriate.

Spearman rank correlation measures the strength and direction of a
monotonic relationship using ranks. It can be useful for ordinal data or
relationships that are monotonic but not linear.

Kendall’s tau is another rank-based measure of ordinal association. It
is based on concordant and discordant pairs.

Do not treat all correlation measures as interchangeable. Choose the
measure based on the data and the type of relationship being studied.

10. COMMON MISUNDERSTANDINGS

Misunderstanding: Covariance must be between -1 and +1. Correction:
Covariance has no fixed range. Correlation is bounded between -1 and +1.

Misunderstanding: A correlation of zero means the variables are
completely unrelated. Correction: It means there is no measured linear
association. A nonlinear relationship may exist.

Misunderstanding: A high correlation proves causation. Correction:
Correlation alone cannot establish cause and effect.

Misunderstanding: Positive covariance means one variable causes the
other to increase. Correction: Positive covariance indicates that the
variables tend to move in the same direction, not that one causes the
other.

Misunderstanding: Covariance and correlation are the same. Correction:
Both describe association, but covariance is scale-dependent while
correlation is standardized.

11. QUICK SUMMARY

Covariance: - Measures the direction of joint variation. - Positive
means variables tend to move together. - Negative means variables tend
to move in opposite directions. - Zero means no linear co-movement is
indicated. - Depends on the variables’ units and scales. - Has no fixed
numerical range.

Correlation: - Measures direction and strength of linear association. -
Positive means a positive linear association. - Negative means a
negative linear association. - Zero means no linear association measured
by the chosen coefficient. - Is dimensionless. - Always ranges from -1
to +1.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Covariance tells us how two variables vary together, including the
direction of their joint movement. Correlation standardizes covariance
so that the direction and strength of a linear relationship can be
interpreted on a common scale from -1 to +1.

Neither covariance nor correlation, by itself, proves causation.
